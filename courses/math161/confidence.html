Confidence Intervals

We now move from the descriptive side of things to inferential statistics. That is, returning to our overarching goal: we want to estimate some \({\color{red}\text{parameter}}\) of the \({\color{red}\text{population}}\), and we do so by obtaining a \({\color{blue}\text{statistic}}\) from a \({\color{blue}\text{sample}}\).

A reasonable question to ask is: \[\text{How }good\text{ is that estimate?}\] We certainly have an intuition that a larger sample leads to a better estimate.

For example, suppose we want to estimate the average number of hours of sleep per night that the residents of a town get.

We might take a sample of 100 residents, and obtain the sample mean \(\overline{x}\) of hours of sleep that those residents get. We say that the \({\color{blue}\text{sample mean } \overline{x}}\) is a point estimate for the \({\color{red}\text{population mean } \mu}\).

Likewise, the \({\color{blue}\text{sample standard deviation } s}\) is a point estimate for the \({\color{red}\text{population standard deviation } \sigma}\).

We can be increasingly confident that our statistic estimates the parameter well as our sample size increases. Suppose we found that the sample mean for hours of sleep was \(\overline{x} = 7\). The idea of a confidence interval is to make statements like \[\text{We are _____% confident that }\mu \text{ is within _____ hours of 7.}\] Clearly, the wider our interval, the greater our confidence level will be.

Suppose we knew the population standard deviation, \(\sigma = 1\), in this example. Since our sample was of size \(n = 100 > 30\), by CLT for sample means, \[\sigma_{\overline{x}} = \frac{\sigma_X}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{100}} = 0.1\] We don't know \(\mu_X\), the population mean hours of sleep: this is what we are trying to estimate. However, we can apply the 68-95-99.7 rule: for instance, we know that 95% of sample means \(\overline{x}\) will land within 2 standard deviations of \(\mu_{\overline{x}}\) (and we have \(\mu_{\overline{x}} = \mu_X\), by CLT for sample means).

This means we can be 95% confident that \(\mu_X\) lies within a distance of \(2 \cdot \sigma_{\overline{x}} = 2 \cdot 0.1 = 0.2\) hours of the sample mean we found.

Since \(7 - 0.2 = 6.8 \text{ and } 7 + 0.2 = 7.2\), \[\text{We are 95% confident that the population average hours of sleep } \mu_X \text{ is between 6.8 and 7.2 hours.}\]

We have just constructed a 95% confidence interval. Such intervals have the form \[(\text{point estimate } - \text{ margin of error}, \text{ point estimate }+\text{ margin of error})\] Note that there are 2 possibilities: there is a 95% chance that the true population mean \(\mu_X\) lies within our interval. So, there is a 5% chance that the sample mean we found is farther than \(0.2\) hours away from the population mean (even if we've followed all the correct guidance to choose an unbiased sample).

"Margin of error" describes the radius of the confidence interval. We see that if we were to widen the confidence interval to 3 standard deviations away (6.7 to 7.3 hours) we improve our confidence level to 99.7%.

From the preceding discussion, it is easy to construct 68%, 95%, and 99.7% confidence intervals for the population mean. We will now learn to construct confidence intervals with any percentage.

First, we will abbreviate the margin of error to EBM (error bound for a population mean), so our confidence intervals for the population mean \(\mu\), given a sample mean \(\overline{x}\) we have found, takes the form \[(\overline{x} - \text{ EBM}, \overline{x} + \text{ EBM})\] A researcher may decide to widen the confidence interval, or increase their sample size, if they want to increase their level of confidence that \(\overline{x}\) accurately estimates \(\mu\) (conversely, a smaller sample size or narrower confidence interval decreases the level of confidence).

In the example we just did, we are 95% confident that our sample mean \(\overline{x} = 7\) lies \({\color{red}\text{within 2 standard deviations of the population mean }\mu}\).


So, the \({\color{blue}\text{area outside 2 standard deviations}}\) occupies \(100-95 = 5\%\) of the area under the normal curve. (the area outside will be called \({\color{blue}\alpha}\))

By the symmetry of the normal distribution, this means \({\color{blue}\frac{\alpha}{2} = \frac{5}{2} =2.5\% \text{ of the area lies on either side}}\) of our \({\color{red}95\% \text{ confidence interval}}\).

So, the left endpoint of our interval has 2.5% of the area of the curve to its left, and the right endpoint of our interval has \(95+2.5 = 97.5\%\) of the area of the curve to its left.

So the problem of finding a 95% confidence interval becomes finding the \(2.5^{\text{th}}\) and \(97.5^{\text{th}}\) percentiles.

Suppose we want to determine the average weight of polar bears. We weigh 64 bears, and find the sample mean weight to be \[\overline{x} = 500 \text{ kg}\] If we know that the population standard deviation of polar bear weights is \(\sigma = 16\) kg, then let us construct an 80% confidence interval of the population mean polar bear weight.

Since we know the population standard deviation \(\sigma = 16\), and this is a question about means, we use the normal distribution for sample means, which itself has mean and standard deviation \[\mu_{\overline{x}} = \mu_X = 500 \hspace{3mm} \text{ and } \hspace{3mm} \sigma_{\overline{x}} = \frac{\sigma_X}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{16}{\sqrt{64}} = 2\]

For an 80% confidence interval, we have \(\alpha = 100\% - 80\% = 20\%\) of the area under the curve outside the interval. Since it is split between either side, we have \(\frac{\alpha}{2} = 10\%\) on either side. We find \(z_{0.1} = -1.28\) and \(z_{0.9} = 1.28\).

So our margin of error is \[\text{EBM }= 1.28(2) = 2.56\] and our confidence interval ranges between \[500 - 2.56 = 497.44 \text{ kg } \hspace{3mm} \text{ and } \hspace{3mm} 500 + 2.56 = 502.56 \text{ kg}\]

Suppose we know that the confidence interval for a population mean is \((17,33)\). We can recover the sample mean and the error bound from this.

The sample mean should be in the middle of this interval, \[\overline{x} = \frac{17+33}{2} = 25\] And so the error bound is the distance from this midpoint to the ends of the confidence interval, \[\text{EBM } = \overline{x} - 17 = 25 - 17 = 8\]

Finally, if we have a desired margin of error and level of confidence, we can determine the sample size we require via the following: \[n = \frac{\left(z_{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\right)^2 \cdot \sigma^2}{(\text{EBM})^2}\] So if we have a study where we want to be 90% confident of our results, to have an error bound of 0.1 kg, and we know that the population standard deviation is 2 kg, then we need a sample of size \[n = \frac{\left(z_{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\right)^2 \cdot \sigma^2}{(\text{EBM})^2} = \frac{\left(z_{\frac{0.1}{2}}\right)^2 \cdot 2^2}{(0.1)^2} = 400 \cdot \left(z_{0.05}\right)^2 = 400 \cdot (-1.64)^2 \approx 1076\]

In the examples we've considered thus far, the population standard deviation \(\sigma\) has been known. In practice, it is most often unknown, and the sample standard deviation \(s\) is used as an estimate for \(\sigma\). However, this runs into problems, especially when the sample size is small.

This is because the CLT tells us that a normal distribution is a good approximation when the sample is large enough: but generally, the distribution is dependent on how large the sample is. For smaller sample sizes, the normal distribution may underestimate how spread out the data are.

So, when we use \(s\) as a stand-in for \(\sigma\), instead of using the normal distribution, we use what is called Student's \(t\)-distribution.

First, the column at left is called "degrees of freedom". When estimating a mean, it is equal to the sample size minus one: \[df = n - 1\] Next, look at the bottom row of the table. \(df = \infty\) means that this row refers to us having an extremely large sample. By CLT, this means the underlying distribution is approximated by a normal distribution.

Essentially, Student's \(t\)-distribution looks increasingly normal as the sample size \(n\) increases. You are encouraged to compare the values in the bottom row of the \(t\)-table with the \(z\)-scores corresponding to the same values \(\frac{\alpha}{2}\): you will see they match up.

The \(t\)-scores function the same as \(z\)-scores: they determine how many standard deviations from the mean we go. The tables function differently, however:

  • In the \(t\)-table, the values in the center of the table are the \(t\)-scores, whereas in the standard normal table, the \(z\)-scores were found using the margins of the table.
  • The \(t\)-table restricts us to only the listed confidence intervals. With the normal table, we were able to construct confidence intervals for arbitrary levels of confidence.
We now give a concrete example. Suppose we want to learn the average grade of the students in a large course by sampling grades from 9 of the students. We learn that their grades are \[\{60, 57, 88, 74, 90, 63, 70, 18, 66\}\] We want to construct a 60% confidence interval for the average grade in the course.

We start by finding the sample mean and sample standard deviation using the methods we've learned previously: \[\overline{x} = \frac{60 + 57 + 88 + 74 + 90 + 63 + 70 + 18 + 66}{9} = \frac{586}{9} = 65.\overline{1} \] \[s = \sqrt{\frac{\sum(x-\overline{x})^2}{n-1}} = \sqrt{\frac{(60-65.\overline{1})^2 + (57 - 65.\overline{1})^2 + ... + (66 - 65.\overline{1})^2}{8}} \approx 21.1\] We use \(s\) as our estimate for \(\sigma\). We then use it to find the standard deviation for the sample mean, \[\sigma_{\overline{x}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{9}} = \frac{21.1}{3} \approx 7.03\] Next, we consult the \(t\)-table for 60% confidence, with \(df = n-1 = 8\) degrees of freedom.

So to construct our 60% confidence interval, we must go \(0.889\) standard deviations from our sample mean: \[65.\overline{1} - 0.889(7.03) \approx 58.86 \hspace{3mm} \text{ and } \hspace{3mm} 65.\overline{1} + 0.889(7.03) = 71.36\]

So we are 60% confident that the average grade in the course is between 58.86% and 71.36%.

As we can see, taking such a small sample makes it hard for us to be particularly confident, and the error bound is quite large.

So far, we have only constructed confidence intervals when the parameter we are interested in estimating is the population mean.

But often, the thing we are interested in estimating is a proportion. For instance, a polling firm may want to estimate the proportion of the population which will vote for a particular candidate.

We have already seen that CLT can be applied to not only sample means, but proportions as well. So our strategy will be very similar to previously.

The main difference is that we won't necessarily be given a proportion to start: the underlying distribution will be binomial.

That is, we will be taking a sample of size \(n\), and obtain a proportion of successes from that sample.

A benefit of the underlying distribution being binomial is that the binomial distribution already bears a close resemblance to the normal distribution: so we will be able to safely approximate with the normal, rather than referring to Student's \(t\)-distribution.

Recall DeMoivre-Laplace: the binomial is approximated by the normal distribution whose mean and standard deviation are the same: \[\mu = np \hspace{3mm} \text{ and } \hspace{3mm} \sigma = \sqrt{np(1-p)}\]

The binomial random variable \(X\) counts the number of successes among the \(n\) trials. So the proportion of successes is the random variable we saw in a version of the CLT, \[\hat{p} = \frac{X}{n},\] whose mean and standard deviation we have already seen (we are just now seeing how they were obtained): \[\mu_{\hat{p}} = \frac{np}{n} = p \hspace{3mm} \text{ and } \hspace{3mm} \sigma_{\hat{p}} = \frac{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}{n} = \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}\] We solve confidence interval problems with sample proportions in the same manner as we did with sample means, only using the above as our mean and standard deviation instead.

Suppose we want to gauge the support of a political party in Nanaimo. We survey 50 people in Nanaimo, and determine that 13 of them support the party. \[p = \frac{13}{50} = 0.26,\] so \[\mu_{\hat{p}} = p = 0.26 \hspace{3mm} \text{ and } \hspace{3mm} \sigma_{\hat{p}} = \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.26(0.74)}{50}} \approx 0.062\] Let us construct a 78% confidence interval of the proportion of the Nanaimo population that will vote for the party.

For a 78% confidence interval, we have \(\alpha = 100\% - 78\% = 22\%\) of the area under the curve outside the interval. Since it is split between either side, we have \(\frac{\alpha}{2} = 11\%\) on either side. We find \(z_{0.11} = -1.23\) and \(z_{0.89} = 1.23\).

So our margin of error is \[\text{EBP } = 1.23(0.062) \approx 0.076\] (EBP standing for error bound for a proportion), and our confidence interval ranges between \[0.26 - 1.23(0.062) \approx 0.184 \hspace{3mm} \text{ and } 0.26 + 1.23(0.062) \approx 0.336\]
We are 78% confident the proportion of people in Nanaimo who will vote for the party is between 18.4% and 33.6%.

This could also be stated as pollsters commonly do: the party is polling at \[26 \pm 7.6\%\] (although note that this statement omits the confidence level of the pollster).

Finally, as was the case for means, if we have a desired margin of error and level of confidence for proportions, we can determine the sample size we require via a slightly different formula (note that we always round up, since we are finding the minimum sample size we need, and we can only have integer sample sizes): \[n = \frac{0.25\left(z_{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\right)^2}{(\text{EBP})^2}\] Suppose in the preceding example, we want to be 99% confident of our polling numbers, with a margin of error of \(\pm0.1\%\). What is the minimum number of people we need to poll to be sure to have this level of certainty? \[\alpha = 0.01 \hspace{3mm} \text{ and } \hspace{3mm} \text{EBP } = 0.001\] So we need to poll at minimum \[n = \frac{0.25\left(z_{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\right)^2}{(\text{EBP})^2} = \frac{0.25(z_{0.005})^2}{0.001^2} = \frac{0.25(2.57)^2}{0.001^2} \approx 1651230 \text{ people}\]
