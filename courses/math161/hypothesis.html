Over the course of their career, a power forward has averaged 5.6 defensive rebounds per game, with a standard deviation of 1 rebound. This season, they have a new coach who has given them new guidance on rebounding. They play in 72 games this season, and average 5.4 defensive rebounds per game. Conduct a hypothesis test at a level of significance of \(\alpha = 0.07\) to determine whether the coach's advice changed their performance. \[H_0: \mu = 5.6 \hspace{3mm} \text{ and } \hspace{3mm} H_a: \mu \neq 5.6\] This is a two-tailed test, since the alternative hypothesis lies in areas on both sides of the mean.

Since we are given the population standard deviation \(\sigma = 1\), and this is a question about means, we use the normal distribution for sample means, which itself has mean and standard deviation \[\mu_{\overline{x}} = \mu_X = 5.6 \hspace{3mm} \text{ and } \hspace{3mm} \sigma_{\overline{x}} = \frac{\sigma_X}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{72}} \approx 0.118\] We find the probability of having a result as extreme (or more) than the one we obtained: that is, being farther than 0.2 rebounds from the mean. \[P(\overline{x} > 5.8 \text{ OR } \overline{x} < 5.4) = 2 \cdot P(\overline{x} < 5.4) = 2 \cdot P\left(Z < \frac{5.4-5.6}{0.118}\right)\] \[= 2 \cdot P(Z < -1.69) = 2(0.0455) = 0.0910\]

Our \(p\)-value \(0.0910 > 0.07 = \alpha\), so we decline to reject \(H_0\). There is insufficient evidence to conclude the new coaching changed performance.

Alternatively, we could find the bounds of rejection in terms of # of standard deviations from the mean. Since \(\alpha = 0.07\), we have 3.5% of the area under the curve in each rejection tail. We look up \(z_{0.035}\), finding \(-1.81\).
Since our result is not as far left as \(-1.81\), at \(-1.69\) standard deviations, we decline to reject \(H_0\).

Or, we find the bounds of the rejection region in terms of # of rebounds: \[5.6-1.81(0.118) = 5.39 \text{ rebounds} \hspace{3mm}\text { and } \hspace{3mm} 5.6+1.81(0.118) = 5.81 \text{ rebounds}\] and our result of \(5.4\) does not make it that far left, so we decline to reject \(H_0\).

Boxes of a particular cereal are advertised as containing more than 67 marshmallows. You buy 5 boxes of the cereal, finding 80, 68, 102, 72, and 89 marshmallows in each of the boxes. Conduct a hypothesis test at a level of significance of \(\alpha = 0.1\) to test the advertised claim.

We start by computing the sample mean and sample standard deviation: \[\overline{x} = \frac{80+68+102+72+89}{5} = 82.2\] \[s = \sqrt{\frac{\sum(x-\overline{x})^2}{n-1}}\] \[= \sqrt{\frac{(80-82.2)^2+(68-82.2)^2+(102-82.2)^2+(72-82.2)^2+(89-82.2)^2}{5-1}} \approx 13.68\] \[H_0: \mu \leq 67 \hspace{3mm} \text{ and } \hspace{3mm} H_a: \mu > 67\] This is a one-tailed (right) test, since the alternative hypothesis lies in an area to the right of the mean.

Since we are using the sample standard deviation \(s = 13.68\) to stand in for the population standard deviation \(\sigma\), we have a small sample size, and this is a question about means, we use Student's \(t\)-distribution for sample means, which itself has mean and standard deviation \[\mu_{\overline{x}} = \mu_X = 82.2 \hspace{3mm} \text{ and } \hspace{3mm} \sigma_{\overline{x}} = \frac{\sigma_X}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{13.68}{\sqrt{5}} = 6.12\] We find the probability of having a result as extreme (or more) than the one we obtained: that is, being farther right than 82.2 marshmallows. \[P(\overline{x} > 82.2) = P\left(t > \frac{82.2-67}{6.12}\right) = P(t > 2.484)\] We can't directly compute probabilities for the \(t\)-distribution, but can find the bounds of the rejection region. First, in terms of # of standard deviations from the mean, since \(\alpha = 0.1\) and \(df = n-1 = 4\), we check the \(t\)-table for a one-tailed test, finding \(t=1.533\).

Our result is farther to the right, at \(2.484\) standard deviations, so we reject \(H_0\). So, there is sufficient evidence to conclude the boxes of cereal do in fact contain more than 67 marshmallows.

Or, we could find the bounds of rejection in terms of # of marshmallows: \[67+1.533(6.12) = 76.38 \text{ marshmallows}\] and our result of \(82.2\) marshallows is farther right than that, so we reject \(H_0\).

You are testing a new vaccine's efficacy. You test 200 members of a community, and find that 3 of them are infected with the relevant virus. Conduct a hypothesis test at a level of significance of \(\alpha = 0.02\) to determine whether the vaccine provides greater than 97% protection.

We can interpret this problem in terms of the proportion of the population infected: \[H_0: \hat{p} \geq 0.03 \hspace{3mm} \text{ and } \hspace{3mm} H_a: \hat{p} < 0.03\] This is a one-tailed test (left), since the alternative hypothesis lies in an area to the left of the mean.

Since this is a question about proportions (we experimentally found \(\hat{p} = \frac{3}{200} = 0.015\)), we use the normal distribution for sample proportions, which itself has mean and standard deviation \[\mu_{\hat{p}} = p = 0.015 \hspace{3mm} \text{ and } \hspace{3mm} \sigma_{\hat{p}} = \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.015(0.985)}{200}} \approx 0.0086\] We find the probability of having a result as extreme (or more) than the one we obtained: that is, being farther left than \(0.015\). \[P(\hat{p} < 0.015) = P\left(Z < \frac{0.015-0.03}{0.0086}\right) = P(Z < -1.74) = 0.0409\]

Our \(p\)-value \(0.0409 > 0.02 = \alpha\), so we decline to reject \(H_0\). There is insufficient evidence to conclude that less than 3% of people are infected.

Alternatively, we could find the bounds of rejection in terms of # of standard deviations from the mean. Since \(\alpha = 0.02\), we have 2% of the area under the curve in the left tail. We look up \(z_{0.02} = -2.05\). Since our result is not that far to the left, at \(-1.74\) standard deviations, we decline to reject \(H_0\).


Or, we find the bounds of the rejection region in terms of proportion: \[0.03-2.05(0.0086) = 0.0124\] and our result of \(0.015\) is not that far to the left, so we decline to reject \(H_0\).

You want to determine whether lemons weigh more than limes, on average. You sample 70 lemons, and they have an average weight of 80g, with a population standard deviation of 7g. You sample 40 limes, and they have an average weight of 78g, with a population standard deviation of 5g. Conduct a hypothesis test at a level of significance of \(\alpha = 0.05\) to determine whether lemons weigh more than limes on average. \[H_0: \mu_c \leq \mu_d \implies \mu_c - \mu_d \leq 0 \hspace{3mm} \text{ and } \hspace{3mm} H_a: \mu_c > \mu_d \implies \mu_c - \mu_d > 0\] Let \(c\) represent lemons and \(d\) represent limes. This is a one-tailed test (right), since the alternative hypothesis lies in an area to the right of the mean.

Since we are given the population standard deviations \(\sigma_c = 7\) and \(\sigma_d = 5\), and this is a question about a difference of means, we use the normal distribution for a difference of sample means, which itself has mean (since the status quo is ``no difference'') and standard deviation \[\mu_{\overline{x}_c - \overline{x}_d} = 0 \hspace{3mm} \text{ and } \hspace{3mm} \sigma_{\overline{x}_c-\overline{x}_d} = \sqrt{\frac{(\sigma_c)^2}{n_c} + \frac{(\sigma_d)^2}{n_d}} = \sqrt{\frac{7^2}{70}+\frac{5^2}{40}} \approx 1.15\] We find the probability of having a result as extreme (or more) than the one we obtained: that is, a difference in weight greater than \(80-78 = 2\) grams. \[P(\overline{x}_c - \overline{x}_d > 3) = 1 - P\left(Z < \frac{2-0}{1.15}\right) = 1 - P(Z < 1.74)\] \[= 1 - 0.9591 = 0.0409\]

Our \(p\)-value \(0.0409 < 0.05 = \alpha\), so we reject \(H_0\). There is sufficient evidence to conclude lemons weigh more than limes on average.

Alternatively, we could find the bounds of rejection in terms of # of standard deviations from the mean. Since \(\alpha = 0.05\), we have 5% of the area under the curve in the right rejection tail. We look up \(z_{0.95} = 1.65\).
Since our result is farther right of \(1.65\), at \(1.74\) standard deviations, we reject \(H_0\).


Or, we find the bounds of the rejection region in terms of grams: \[0+1.65(1.15) = 1.9 \text{ grams}\] and our result of 2 grams is farther right than that, so we reject \(H_0\).

Two teachers believe the students in their classes perform approximately the same on tests. The 7 students in Class A had an average grade of 80% on a test, with a standard deviation of 5%. The 12 students in Class B had an average grade of 75% on the same test, with a standard deviation of 7%. Conduct a hypothesis test at a level of significance of \(\alpha = 0.05\) to determine whether there is a significant difference between the classes' performance on tests. \[H_0: \mu_A = \mu_B \implies \mu_A - \mu_B = 0 \hspace{3mm} \text{ and } \hspace{3mm} H_a: \mu_A \neq \mu_B \implies \mu_A - \mu_B \neq 0\] This is a two-tailed test, since the alternative hypothesis lies in areas on both sides of the mean.

Since we don't know the population standard deviations (and are using sample standard deviations \(s_A = 0.05\) and \(s_B = 0.07\) to stand in for \(\sigma_A\) and \(\sigma_B\)), and this is a question about a difference of means, we use Student's \(t\)-distribution for a difference of sample means, which itself has mean (since the status quo is "no difference") and standard deviation \[\mu_{\overline{x}_A - \overline{x}_B} = 0 \hspace{3mm} \text{ and } \hspace{3mm} \sigma_{\overline{x}_A-\overline{x}_B} = \sqrt{\frac{(\sigma_A)^2}{n_A} + \frac{(\sigma_B)^2}{n_B}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.05^2}{7}+\frac{0.07^2}{12}} \approx 0.0277\] We find the probability of having a result as extreme (or more) than the one we obtained: that is, a difference of more than \(80\%-75\% = 0.05\) in grades. \[P(\overline{x}_A - \overline{x}_B > 0.05 \text{ OR } \overline{x}_A - \overline{x}_B < -0.05) = 2 \cdot P\left(t > \frac{0.05-0}{0.0277}\right) = 2 \cdot P(t > 1.81)\] We can't directly compute probabilities for the \(t\)-distribution, but can find the bounds of the rejection region. First, we find \[df = \frac{\left(\frac{(s_A)^2}{n_A} + \frac{(s_B)^2}{n_B}\right)^2}{\left(\frac{1}{n_A-1}\right)\left(\frac{(s_A)^2}{n_A}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{1}{n_B-1}\right)\left(\frac{(s_B)^2}{n_B}\right)^2} = \frac{\left(\frac{0.05^2}{7} + \frac{0.07^2}{12}\right)^2}{\left(\frac{1}{7-1}\right)\left(\frac{0.05^2}{7}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{1}{12-1}\right)\left(\frac{0.07^2}{12}\right)^2}\] \[\approx 16.09\] so we round up and consult the \(t\)-table for a two-tailed test, \(\alpha = 0.05\), \(df = 17\), finding \(t = 2.11\).

Our result is not that far to the right, at \(1.81\) standard deviations, so we decline to reject \(H_0\). So, there is insufficient evidence to conclude there is a significant difference in the classes' performance on tests.


Or, we could find the bounds of rejection in terms of grades: \[0+2.11(0.0277) = 0.058447 \] and our result of \(0.05\) is not that far to the right, so we decline to reject \(H_0\).

You want to determine whether the Green party is more popular with vegetarians. You survey 100 vegetarians, 30 of whom say they plan to vote for the Green party. You survey 120 non-vegetarians, 21 of whom say they plan to vote for the Green party. Conduct a hypothesis test at a level of significance of \(\alpha = 0.02\) to see whether the Green party is more popular with vegetarians. \[H_0: \mu_v \leq \mu_m \implies \mu_v - \mu_m \leq 0 \hspace{3mm} \text{ and } \hspace{3mm} H_a: \mu_v > \mu_m \implies \mu_v - \mu_m > 0\] This is a one-tailed test (right), since the alternative hypothesis lies in an area to the right of the mean.

Since this is a question about a difference of proportions (we experimentally found \(\hat{p}_v = \frac{30}{100} = 0.3\) and \(\hat{p}_m = \frac{21}{120} = 0.175\)), we use the normal distribution for a difference of sample proportions, which itself has mean (since the status quo is "no difference") and standard deviation \[\mu_{\hat{p}_v - \hat{p}_m} = 0 \hspace{3mm} \text{ and } \hspace{3mm} \sigma_{\hat{p}_v-\hat{p}_m} = \sqrt{\left(\frac{x_v+x_m}{n_v+n_m}\right)\left(1 - \frac{x_v+x_m}{n_v+n_m}\right)\left(\frac{1}{n_v} + \frac{1}{n_m}\right)}\] \[=\sqrt{\left(\frac{30+21}{100+120}\right)\left(1 - \frac{30+21}{100+120}\right)\left(\frac{1}{100} + \frac{1}{120}\right)} \approx 0.0571\] We find the probability of having a result as extreme (or more) than the one we obtained: that is, a difference in proportion greater than \(0.3-0.175 = 0.125\). \[P(\hat{p}_v - \hat{p}_m > 0.125) = 1 - P\left(Z < \frac{0.125-0}{0.0571}\right) = 1 - P(Z < 2.19)\] \[= 1 - 0.9857 = 0.0143\]

Our \(p\)-value \(0.0143 < 0.02 = \alpha\), so we reject \(H_0\). There is sufficient evidence to conclude the Green party is more popular with vegetarians.

Alternatively, we could find the bounds of rejection in terms of # of standard deviations from the mean. Since \(\alpha = 0.02\), we have 2% of the area under the curve in the right rejection tail. We look up \(z_{0.98} = 2.05\).
Since our result is farther right of \(2.05\), at \(2.19\) standard deviations, we reject \(H_0\).


Or, we find the bounds of the rejection region in terms of years: \[0+2.05(0.0571) = 0.117055 \text{ years}\] and our result of \(0.125\) is farther right than that, so we reject \(H_0\).

Four students (Bart, Milhouse, Ralph, and Nelson) start taking the drug Focusyn to improve their ability to detect conspiracies. Before taking Focusyn, in an average month, they detected 3, 1, 0, and 1 respectively. After Focusyn, they detected 7, 9, 5, and 13 respectively. Conduct a hypothesis test at a level of significance of \(\alpha = 0.01\) to determine whether Focusyn improves one's ability to uncover conspiracies.

We start by finding the differences (they are our data -- this is a paired \(t\)-test since we have two measurements from the same sample): \[\{7-3,9-1,5-0,13-1\} = \{4,8,5,12\}\] and then computing the sample mean and sample standard deviation: \[\overline{x} = \frac{4+8+5+12}{4} = 7.25\] \[s = \sqrt{\frac{\sum(x-\overline{x})^2}{n-1}} = \sqrt{\frac{(4-7.25)^2+(8-7.25)^2+(5-7.25)^2+(12-7.25)^2}{4-1}} \approx 3.594\] \[H_0: \mu \leq 0 \hspace{3mm} \text{ and } \hspace{3mm} H_a: \mu > 0\] This is a one-tailed (right) test, since the alternative hypothesis lies in an area to the right of the mean.

Since we are using the sample standard deviation \(s = 3.594\) to stand in for the population standard deviation \(\sigma\), we have a small sample size, and this is a question about means, we use Student's \(t\)-distribution for sample means, which itself has mean (since the status quo is "no difference") and standard deviation \[\mu_{\overline{x}} = 0 \hspace{3mm} \text{ and } \hspace{3mm} \sigma_{\overline{x}} = \frac{\sigma_X}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{3.594}{\sqrt{4}} = 1.797\] We find the probability of having a result as extreme (or more) than the one we obtained: that is, being farther right than 9.1 hours. \[P(\overline{x} > 7.25) = P\left(t > \frac{7.25-0}{1.797}\right) = P(t > 4.03)\] We can't directly compute probabilities for the \(t\)-distribution, but can find the bounds of the rejection region. First, in terms of # of standard deviations from the mean, since \(\alpha = 0.01\) and \(df = n-1 = 3\), we check the \(t\)-table for a one-tailed test, finding \(t=4.541\).

Our result is not quite that far to the right, at \(4.03\) standard deviations, so we decline to reject \(H_0\). So, there is insufficient evidence to conclude Focusyn improves conspiracy detection ability.


Or, we could find the bounds of rejection in terms of # of conspiracies: \[0+4.541(1.797) = 8.160177 \text{ conspiracies}\] and our result of \(7.25\) hours is not that far to the right, so we decline to reject \(H_0\).
