Over the course of their career, a power forward has averaged 5.6 defensive rebounds per game, with a standard deviation of 1 rebound. This season, they have a new coach who has given them new guidance on rebounding. They play in 72 games this season, and average 5.4 defensive rebounds per game. Conduct a hypothesis test at a level of significance of \(\alpha = 0.07\) to determine whether the coach's advice changed their performance. \[H_0: \mu = 5.6 \hspace{3mm} \text{ and } \hspace{3mm} H_a: \mu \neq 5.6\] This is a two-tailed test, since the alternative hypothesis lies in areas on both sides of the mean.

Since we are given the population standard deviation \(\sigma = 1\), and this is a question about means, we use the normal distribution for sample means, which itself has mean and standard deviation \[\mu_{\overline{x}} = \mu_X = 5.6 \hspace{3mm} \text{ and } \hspace{3mm} \sigma_{\overline{x}} = \frac{\sigma_X}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{72}} \approx 0.118\] We find the probability of having a result as extreme (or more) than the one we obtained: that is, being farther than 0.2 rebounds from the mean. \[P(\overline{x} > 5.8 \text{ OR } \overline{x} < 5.4) = 2 \cdot P(\overline{x} < 5.4) = 2 \cdot P\left(Z < \frac{5.4-5.6}{0.118}\right)\] \[= 2 \cdot P(Z < -1.69) = 2(0.0455) = 0.0910\] INSERT FIGURE HERE Our \(p\)-value \(0.0910 > 0.07 = \alpha\), so we decline to reject \(H_0\). There is insufficient evidence to conclude the new coaching changed performance.

Alternatively, we could find the bounds of rejection in terms of # of standard deviations from the mean. Since \(\alpha = 0.07\), we have 3.5% of the area under the curve in each rejection tail. Boxes of a particular cereal are advertised as containing more than 67 marshmallows. You buy 5 boxes of the cereal, finding 80, 68, 102, 72, and 89 marshmallows in each of the boxes. Conduct a hypothesis test at a level of significance of \(\alpha = 0.1\) to test the advertised claim.

We start by computing the sample mean and sample standard deviation: \[\overline{x} = \frac{80+68+102+72+89}{5} = 82.2\] \[s = \sqrt{\frac{\sum(x-\overline{x})^2}{n-1}}\] \[= \sqrt{\frac{(80-82.2)^2+(68-82.2)^2+(102-82.2)^2+(72-82.2)^2+(89-82.2)^2}{5-1}} \approx 13.68\] \[H_0: \mu \leq 67 \hspace{3mm} \text{ and } \hspace{3mm} H_a: \mu > 67\] This is a one-tailed (right) test, since the alternative hypothesis lies in an area to the right of the mean.

Since we are using the sample standard deviation \(s = 13.68\) to stand in for the population standard deviation \(\sigma\), we have a small sample size, and this is a question about means, we use Student's \(t\)-distribution for sample means, which itself has mean and standard deviation \[\mu_{\overline{x}} = \mu_X = 82.2 \hspace{3mm} \text{ and } \hspace{3mm} \sigma_{\overline{x}} = \frac{\sigma_X}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{13.68}{\sqrt{5}} = 6.12\] We find the probability of having a result as extreme (or more) than the one we obtained: that is, being farther right than 82.2 marshmallows. \[P(\overline{x} > 82.2) = P\left(t > \frac{82.2-67}{6.12}\right) = P(t > 2.484)\] We can't directly compute probabilities for the \(t\)-distribution, but can find the bounds of the rejection region. First, in terms of # of standard deviations from the mean, since \(\alpha = 0.1\) and \(df = n-1 = 4\), we check the \(t\)-table for a one-tailed test, finding \(t=1.533\). INSERT FIGURE HERE Our result is farther to the right, at \(2.484\) standard deviations, so we reject \(H_0\). So, there is sufficient evidence to conclude the boxes of cereal do in fact contain more than 67 marshmallows.

You are testing a new vaccine's efficacy. You test 200 members of a community, and find that 3 of them are infected with the relevant virus. Conduct a hypothesis test at a level of significance of \(\alpha = 0.02\) to determine whether the vaccine provides greater than 97% protection.

We can interpret this problem in terms of the proportion of the population infected: \[H_0: \hat{p} \geq 0.03 \hspace{3mm} \text{ and } \hspace{3mm} H_a: \hat{p} < 0.03\] This is a one-tailed test (left), since the alternative hypothesis lies in an area to the left of the mean.

Since this is a question about proportions (we experimentally found \(\hat{p} = \frac{3}{200} = 0.015\)), we use the normal distribution for sample proportions, which itself has mean and standard deviation \[\mu_{\hat{p}} = p = 0.015 \hspace{3mm} \text{ and } \hspace{3mm} \sigma_{\hat{p}} = \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.015(0.985)}{200}} \approx 0.0086\] We find the probability of having a result as extreme (or more) than the one we obtained: that is, being farther left than \(0.015\). \[P(\hat{p} < 0.015) = P\left(Z < \frac{0.015-0.03}{0.0086}\right) = P(Z < -1.74) = 0.0409\] INSERT FIGURE HERE Our \(p\)-value \(0.0409 > 0.02 = \alpha\), so we decline to reject \(H_0\). There is insufficient evidence to conclude that less than 3% of people are infected.
